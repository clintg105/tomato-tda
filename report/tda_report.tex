\PaperTitle{Tomato TDA: Building an Entropy–Lifetime Atlas of Text Embeddings}
\Abstract{
We analyze 270 finite metric spaces derived from movie‐review text by combining three embedding schemes (BoW, TF–IDF, BERT) with six distance constructions (cosine, $\ell_{1}$, $\ell_{\infty}$, Sinkhorn–Wasserstein, geodesic $k$-NN, and their hybrids).  For each space we build a Vietoris–Rips filtration and extract $H_{0,1,2}$ barcodes via cohomology‐based reduction in Ripser \cite{bauer2021ripser}.  To compare diagrams we (i) normalize to unit diameter, (ii) compute total persistence and persistence entropy, (iii) vectorize via landscapes and images, and (iv) evaluate four contrast measures ($W_1$, bottleneck, $\ell_2$‐landscapes, persistence images).  We add a small entropy penalty to the transport plan to make Sinkhorn‐Wasserstein computation tractable on dense BERT token clouds \cite{cuturi2013sinkhorn}, and we include $k$-NN‐graph geodesic distances to examine how manifold‐aware metrics affect persistence \cite{tenenbaum2000global}. \\
Our reproducible pipeline runs end-to-end in under 5 hours on a laptop and yields an “entropy–lifetime” atlas to guide metric selection for topological data analysis. This emphasizes and illustrates that, in topology, above all else, your choice of open sets is what matters most.
}

\section{Introduction}
Topological Data Analysis (TDA) has emerged as a powerful framework for analyzing complex, high-dimensional data by capturing its shape and structure using tools from algebraic topology. While TDA has found success in various domains including image analysis, genomics, and material science, its application to natural language processing (NLP) and text analysis remains relatively unexplored.

This study investigates the potential of TDA for understanding and classifying movie reviews. Reviews constitute a rich domain of study as they combine objective descriptions, subjective opinions, and stylistic elements that may be captured by topological features. Our work is motivated by several key questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Can topological features of text embeddings effectively distinguish between different categories of movie reviews (e.g., by genre, sentiment, or critic)?
    \item How do different text embedding methods (Bag of Words, TF-IDF, BERT) and distance metrics influence the resulting topological signatures?
    \item Can persistent homology reveal meaningful patterns in review text that traditional NLP approaches might miss?
\end{itemize}

We present a framework that combines modern NLP embedding techniques with computational topology. By analyzing 54 combinations of embeddings and metrics across 270 metric spaces, we systematically evaluate which approaches best capture the semantic and structural information in review text. Our methodology leverages persistent homology to extract topological signatures that characterize the "shape" of the data in embedding space, then applies various metrics to quantify differences between these signatures.

The remainder of this report is structured as follows: In the Methodology section, we describe our software tools, dataset, embedding methods, and the various distance metrics used in our analysis. Next, we explore the topological structures discovered in our data and present insights on their persistence and entropy characteristics. We then evaluate how well different embedding-metric combinations discriminate between review categories. The report concludes with a summary of our key findings and potential directions for future work in applying TDA to text analysis.

We began by loading the raw kaggle review data into pandas DataFrames \cite{kaggle_rotten_tomatoes, mckinney2010pandas}. Text was cleaned, tokenized, POS-tagged, and lemmatized with NLTK \cite{bird2009nltk}. Documents were encoded with bag-of-words and TF-IDF using scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, while in parallel we generated BERT token embeddings via HuggingFace Transformers and PyTorch \cite{wolf2020transformers,paszke2019pytorch}. Embeddings and feature matrices were stored as NumPy arrays for downstream processing \cite{harris2020array}. We then built sparse distance graphs and boundary matrices with SciPy and scikit-learn utilities \cite{virtanen2020scipy,pedregosa2011scikit}, approximating geodesic distances on the inferred manifold by constructing k-nearest-neighbor graphs and computing all-pairs shortest paths \cite{virtanen2020scipy}.\\
From each boundary matrix we formed a Vietoris–Rips filtration and extracted persistence barcodes using Ripser \cite{bauer2021ripser}. Persistence diagrams were post-processed and plotted with persim \cite{persim2024persim}. Finally, we measured Wasserstein distances between diagrams with the POT toolbox, using both Sinkhorn regularization \cite{cuturi2013sinkhorn} and sliced approximations \cite{bonneel2015sliced,flamary2021pot}. \subsection{Real Embeddings}
We mostly consider a single reviews to be single points embedded in $\mathbb{R}^d$, except for when we use metrics based on \nameref{ws}.
\subsection{Metric Spaces}
To apply TDA, we require a notion of distance between points (reviews) in our feature space, since persistent homology typically operates on a metric space \cite{edelsbrunner2009computational}.
\subsubsection{Cosine Distance}
Given two vectors $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^d$, we obtain their angle using the dot product $\langle x, y\rangle  = |x||y|\cos(\theta).$
This is converted to a distance via 
$$d_{\cos}(x,y) = \sqrt{2\left(1 - \cos(\theta)\right)} \in [0,2].$$
Cosine distance focuses on the difference in direction of the vectors, effectively comparing the relative term weights. For TF–IDF or BoW, this is useful because it reduces the influence of document length or overall word frequency. 
\subsubsection{Minkowski Distances}
A broad family of distances defined as 
$$d_p(x,y) = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^d |x_i - y_i|^p \Big)^{1/p}$$
for $p\ge1$ satisfy the metric axioms \cite{viro2008elementary}. We consider two important cases:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Manhattan distance} ($\ell_1$ norm, with $p=1$)
	$$d_{1}(x,y) = \sum_{i}|x_i - y_i|$$
	If $x,y$ are BoW vectors, $d_1$ counts the total difference in word counts between two reviews. This can be more robust than Euclidean ($p=2$) to high-dimensional sparse differences, since it doesn’t overly emphasize large single-component differences (instead, it treats each dimension linearly).
	\item \textbf{Chebyshev distance} ($\ell_{\infty}$ norm, the limit as $p \to \infty$)
	$$d_{\infty}(x,y) = \max_i |x_i - y_i|$$
	In text, $d_{\infty}$ might be interpreted as looking at the word with the most disproportionate frequency between two reviews. For example, if one review heavily uses the word “excellent” and another does not use it at all, Chebyshev distance would equal the normalized frequency difference for "excellent".
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Entropic-Regularized Optimal Transport}\label{ws}
Consider a review embedded as a series of BERT tokens. Suppose if instead of pooling the tokens into a vector, we represented each review as an empirical distribution, $P = \{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^{m}$ or $Q = \{\mathbf{y}_j\}_{j=1}^{n}$ over our feature space. The Wasserstein distance (Earth Mover’s distance) between $P$ and $Q$ is defined as the minimum “cost” to transport the mass of distribution $P$ onto $Q$ \cite{villani2008optimal}. If the ground cost between points is taken to be \textit{any of the above metrics} $d(x,y)$, the $p$-Wasserstein distance is
$$
W_p(P, Q)=\left(\min _{\pi \in \Pi(P, Q)} \sum_{i, j} \pi_{i j} d\left(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_j\right)^p\right)^{1 / p}
$$
where $\Pi(P,Q)$ is the set of all couplings (or transport plans) $\pi$ with $\pi_{ij}\ge0$, $\sum_j \pi_{ij} = 1/m$ (supply at $\mathbf{x}_i$) and $\sum_i \pi_{ij} = 1/n$ (demand at $\mathbf{y}_j$). This optimal transport (OT) problem can be solved by linear programming \cite{flamary2021pot}, but this became computationally intractable for large $m,n$.\\
Cuturi \cite{cuturi2013sinkhorn} proposed adding an entropy penalty to the OT problem to make it computationally efficient via iterative matrix scaling (Sinkhorn’s algorithm). The Sinkhorn–Wasserstein distance (also called Sinkhorn divergence when made symmetric) introduces a small $\epsilon > 0$ and solves
$$
W_p^{(\epsilon)}(P, Q)=\left(\min _{\pi \in \Pi(P, Q)} \sum_{i, j} \pi_{i j} d\left(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_j\right)^p+\epsilon \sum_{i, j} \pi_{i j} \ln \pi_{i j}\right)^{1 / p} .
$$
The extra entropy term $H(\pi) = -\sum_{ij}\pi_{ij}\ln \pi_{ij}$ encourages the optimal $\pi$ to be more diffuse (higher entropy), avoiding overly sparse solutions and greatly speeding up computation via iterative proportional fitting (the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm) \cite{cuturi2013sinkhorn}. In this work, we used a typically computed $W_1^{(0.01)}$ unless explicitly stated otherwise.\\
For comparing text embedding distributions, $W_p$ gives a meaningful measure: if two sets of reviews have very similar content and style distribution, the Wasserstein distance will be low (cheap transport), whereas if one set of reviews uses very different language (requiring moving probability mass far in the embedding space), the distance will be high.
\subsubsection{Graph-Based Geodesic Distances}
Imagine for a moment that our movie-review embeddings aren't just scattered in $\mathbb{R}^D$ but actually lie on some hidden, winding "topic manifold" $M$. We can approximate the structure of this manifold by building the $k$-nearest-neighbor graph $G$ on our points $\left\{x_i\right\}$, weighing each edge by \textit{any of the above metrics} $d\left(x_i, x_j\right)$, and defining
$$
d_G\left(x_i, x_j\right)=\min _{\pi: i \rightarrow j} \sum_{(u, v) \in \pi} d(u, v)
$$
where the optimal path $\hat{\pi}: i \rightarrow j$ is computed via Dijkstra's or Floyd-Warshall in SciPy \cite{virtanen2020scipy}. Under mild sampling conditions,
$$
d_G\left(x_i, x_j\right) \xrightarrow[k \rightarrow \infty]{ } d_{M}\left(x_i, x_j\right)=\inf _{\gamma: i \rightarrow j} \int_0^1\left\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\right\| d t
$$
up to a small error term $\mathcal{O}(h)$, where $h \approx \max _i \min _{j \neq i}\left\|x_i-x_j\right\|$ is the sampling radius \cite{tenenbaum2000global}.\\
Intuitively, if two reviews are not directly similar (in Euclidean terms) but have a chain of intermediate reviews linking them (each similar to the next), then $d_G$ will reflect the sum of small hops, capturing that they are connected through a topic pathway.

\subsection{Topological Structure}
\subsubsection*{Process}
We follow the standard TDA procedure:
\[
(X,d)\xrightarrow{\tau_d}(X,\tau_d)
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{VR}}\{\mathrm{VR}_\alpha(X)\}_{\alpha\ge0}
\xrightarrow{H_k}\mathrm{Dgm}_k(X),
\]
where
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item From the metric, sets $B(x;r)=\{y:d(x,y)<r\}$ generate
  \[
    \tau_d=\{U\subset X:\forall x\in U\,\exists r>0\,B(x;r)\subset U\}.
  \]
  inducing the open-ball topology \cite{viro2008elementary,edelsbrunner2014short}
  \item The Vietoris-Rips filtration
  $$
  \mathrm{VR}_\alpha(X)=\left\{\sigma \subset X: \max _{u, v \in \sigma} d(u, v) \leq \alpha\right\}
  $$
  forms nested simplicial complexes \cite{zomorodian2005topology}.
  \item Filtered  boundary matrices define the homology functor
  \begin{align*}
  H_k(&\mathrm{VR}_\alpha(X);\mathbb{F}_2)=\ker\partial_k/\mathrm{im}\,\partial_{k+1},\quad\text{with}\\
  &\partial_k([v_0,\dots,v_k])=\sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^i[v_0,\dots,\widehat v_i,\dots,v_k],
  \end{align*}
  \cite{edelsbrunner2009computational,edelsbrunner2014short}. Then Ripser’s cohomology‐based matrix-reduction algorithm is used to produce birth-death intervals $[b, d)$ \cite{bauer2021ripser}.
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Persistence Diagrams}
The persistence diagram 
$$
\operatorname{Dgm}_k(X)=\left\{\left(b_i, d_i\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N_k}.
$$
is a complete invariant of the homology of the Vietoris-Rips filtration $\mathrm{VR}_\alpha(X)$, as it encodes the birth and death of topological features at various scales, uniquely characterizing the $k$-dimensional homology of $X$.
\subsubsection*{Betti Curves}
Betti curves plot the rank of homology groups
\begin{align*}
	\beta_k(\alpha)&=\operatorname{rank}H_k(\mathrm{VR}_\alpha(X);\mathbb{F}_2)\\
	&=\sum_{(b_i,d_i)\in\operatorname{Dgm}_k(X)}\!\mathbf 1_{[\,b_i,d_i)}(\alpha)\qquad 0\le\alpha\le\alpha_{\max}.
\end{align*}
as a function of the scale parameter $\alpha$, which corresponds to the parameter in the Vietoris-Rips filtration

\subsubsection*{Normalization}
In order to compare across metrics and emphasize relative topology, we need to correct for the fact that every metric will have a distinct absolute scale.\\
For a metric-space persistence diagram $\operatorname{Dgm}_k(X)$ with 
$$\operatorname{diam}(X)=\max _{i, j} d\left(x_i, x_j\right),$$
we normalize to $\widetilde{\operatorname{Dgm}}_k(X)$ via
$$
\left(b_i, d_i\right) \mapsto\left(b_i / \operatorname{diam}(X), d_i / \operatorname{diam}(X)\right).
$$
Stability under scalar multiplication ensures consistency and permits rescaling after diagram computation \cite{cohen2007stability}. Moreover, this extends naturally to Betti curves $\tilde{\beta}_k(u)=\beta_k\bigl(u\cdot\operatorname{diam}(X)\bigr)$.

\section{Full Structure}
\subsection{Quantifying Structure}
On each normalized diagram we compute total persistance and persistant entropy,
\begin{align*}
	\operatorname{TP}_k(X)&=\sum_{i}\left(d_i-b_i\right)=\int_0^{\alpha_{\max }} \tilde{\beta}_k(\alpha) d \alpha\\
	\operatorname{E}_k(X)&=\sum_{i}-p_i\log(p_i), \quad p_i=\frac{d_i-b_i}{\sum_j\left(d_j-b_j\right)}
\end{align*}
where each index above is taken to be over $\left(b_i, d_i\right) \in \widetilde{\operatorname{Dgm}}_k(X)$. Together these measures capture overall lifetime and dispersion of features \cite{adams2017persistence}. Aggregating dimension indices implies summation.

\subsubsection*{Embedding/Metric Interactions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{BERT magnifies Chebyshev extremes.}  
  Contextual tokens give BERT dense, high-range coordinates.  
  Under $d_\infty$ even one rare word (say, \emph{“transcendent”}) stretches distances for every review that contains it.  
  Add plain Wasserstein and the coupling plan sums that gap over dozens of matches, so BERT + $d_\infty$ + $W_1$ produces a skyline of bars an order of magnitude taller than anything else.
\item \textbf{BoW and TF–IDF reward cosine angles.}  
  These sparse count vectors differ mostly in direction, not magnitude.  
  Cosine therefore pumps up lifetimes while keeping entropy tiny; the ratio $\eta$ shoots past 15, but the bars never exceed a few hundred units, far below BERT’s Chebyshev tower.
\item \textbf{10-NN graph trimming hurts height everywhere.}  
  Replacing raw distances with geodesic paths cuts long edges; BERT loses two decades, Cosine families lose about 15\%.  
  Entropy often rises slightly (bars bunch into a tighter range) so $\eta$ falls.
\item \textbf{$\ell_1$ is consistently flat.}  
  Averaging coordinates suppresses extremes.  
  Whether plain or graph-corrected, $d_1$ fails to crack ten units of height in any embedding, leaving both $\tilde T$ and $\eta$ at the bottom.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Key observations}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Amplitude first.}  If you need bars tall enough for any distance metric to grab, choose \emph{BERT with Chebyshev and no graph trimming}.  One extreme token can lift an entire skyline.
\item \textbf{Compactness for resampling.}  When you prefer tight, low-variance diagrams (e.g.\ for bootstrapped estimates), \emph{Cosine on sparse vectors} gives the best height per entropy bit, but the overall structure stays shallow.
\item \textbf{Graph corrections trade height for sharpness.}  Adding a $k$-NN geodesic metric removes long edges, shortening every bar.  The picture gets cleaner, but you lose amplitude.
\end{itemize}

These patterns set the baseline.  When we start measuring class differences we will see how much of that raw height actually turns into separability.

\section{Discriminating Structure}


\subsection{Quantifying Contrasts}
In this section we use abbreviations $D,D'$ for distinct normalized diagrams, $\widetilde{\operatorname{Dgm}}_k$.
\subsubsection{Wasserstein-1}

Interprets each diagram as a mass distribution and computes the minimal $\ell^1$ transport cost. We have
$$
W_1(D,D') \;=\; \inf_{\gamma} \sum_{p \in D} \bigl\lVert p - \gamma(p)\bigr\rVert_{1},
$$
where the infimum is over all bijections $\gamma\colon D\to D'$ (allowing matches to the diagonal).

\subsubsection{Bottleneck Distance}

Finds the pairing that minimizes the maximal $\ell^\infty$ displacement, ensuring stability under small perturbations \cite{cohen2007stability},
$$
d_B(D,D') \;=\; \inf_{\gamma}\;\sup_{p \in D} \bigl\lVert p - \gamma(p)\bigr\rVert_{\infty}.
$$

\subsubsection*{Vectorization}
Both methods below are vectorizations embedding diagrams into a Hilbert space for standard ML methods \cite{bubenik2015statistical,adams2017persistence}. One we represent diagrams $D,D'$ as vectors, we simply take the euclidean distance, $d_2$.

\subsubsection{Persistence Landscapes}
Transform each interval $(b_i,d_i)$ into a symmetric “tent” function, 
$$
f_{b_i,d_i}(t)=\max\bigl(0,\min\{t-b_i,\;d_i-t\}\bigr).
$$
Then at each time $t$ take the $k$-th largest tent height
$$
\lambda_k(t)=\text{the $k$-th largest of }\{f_{b_i,d_i}(t)\}_i,\quad k\ge1.
$$
to form a sequence $\Lambda(D)=(\lambda_k)_{k\ge1}\in\ell^2$, yielding a stable, Hilbert-space embedding that hierarchically encodes feature prominence \cite{bubenik2015statistical}. \\

\subsubsection{Persistence Images}

Map each $(b,d)\in D$ to $(x,y)=(b,d-b)$ with weight $w(b,y)$, then place Gaussian kernels $K_\sigma((u,v),(x,y))$ and rasterize on grid $(u_p,v_q)$:
$$
I_{p,q}=\sum_{(b_i,d_i)\in D}w(b_i,d_i-b_i)\;K_\sigma\bigl((u_p,v_q),(b_i,d_i-b_i)\bigr).
$$
Flattening $\{I_{p,q}\}$ yields a finite-dimensional embedding of the diagram \cite{adams2017persistence}.

\subsubsection*{Overview of Top Metrics}
Table \ref{tab:best-metrics} summarizes leading results. BERT with the Chebyshev metric combined with the standard Wasserstein distance attains the highest combined score ($012$) in every split, although the magnitude of its lead differs:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Content rating} is the most straightforward task: a score of 347.6 substantially exceeds the next best. Terms with strong moral connotations (e.g.\ “violent,” “wholesome”) produce pronounced extremes in the feature space, yielding early persistent loops.
  \item \textbf{Critic name} follows at 211.0. Individual stylistic features function similarly to moral vocabulary but exhibit greater overlap across critics, narrowing the margin.
  \item \textbf{Genre (drama vs comedy)} and \textbf{Sentiment (fresh vs rotten)} both score around 170. These rely on more diffuse lexical shifts—no single token dominates—so the advantage of the Chebyshev metric is more modest.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Relative performance of contrast methods}
Averaging across all pairings (Table \ref{tab:disc-avg}) offers a consolidated comparison:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The $1$-Wasserstein distance consistently outperforms other measures, though its relative gain diminishes as tasks become easier. Shifting from “genre” to “critic name” increases the mean score by a factor of 2.3, mirroring the median increase from Cosine to Chebyshev persistence.
  \item The bottleneck distance plateaus near 0.2 for every task; it captures only the largest single mismatch and overlooks the accumulation of loops.
  \item Persistence landscapes and images never exceed 0.5. Their truncation and smoothing obscure the tail of long-lived features that BERT+$d_{\infty}$ exploits.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Embedding–metric alignment}
Comparing these findings with the baseline embeddings (Table \ref{tab:emb-metric}) clarifies which geometry each category favors:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Content rating} benefits most from the highest-magnitude distances: BERT with Chebyshev. A small set of age-restriction adjectives induces large separations, so all PG reviews interconnect before any contact with R-rated reviews.
  \item \textbf{Critic name} also leverages extreme distances, albeit to a lesser extent, so the Chebyshev-induced “height” remains informative without overwhelming.
  \item \textbf{Genre} and \textbf{Sentiment} extract value from $d_{\infty}$ but can operate—if necessary—using Cosine distance with a graph-based filtration; the performance drop is roughly 4x rather than 20x.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Rationale for loop dominance}
Under BERT+$d_{\infty}$+$W_1$, the average joint score decomposes as
$$
H_1 = 0.86,\quad H_0 = 0.12,\quad H_2 = 0.02.
$$
Loops dominate because all four categories are keyed to recurring phrases rather than isolated clusters or rare voids. We can look at this another way by comparing BERT and BoW.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{BoW} vectors are nearly binary.  
The largest coordinate difference is a word present in one review and absent in the other, creating many late–merging components; loops seldom form, so $H_0$ dominates.
\item \textbf{BERT} embeddings are dense.  
The maximal‐coordinate gap now corresponds to a salient semantic axis shared pairwise but not jointly, producing persistent 1–cycles; components merge early, so $H_1$ carries the signal.
\end{itemize}
Thus Chebyshev+$W_1$ extracts cluster separation from sparse BoW ($H_0$) but extracts recurrent semantic loops from dense BERT ($H_1$); higher‐dimensional voids remain negligible in both cases.
\subsubsection*{Key conclusions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Use \textbf{BERT + Chebyshev + $W_1$} when the category depends on sparse, distinctive tokens (e.g.\ content rating, critic idiolect).
  \item You may \textbf{downgrade to Cosine distance} (with a 10-NN filtration) for genre or sentiment if simpler persistence diagrams are preferred; the separation margin contracts but remains significant.
  \item Do not rely on bottleneck distance, persistence landscapes, or persistence images for these text categorization tasks; none capture the long-lived feature tail sufficiently.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Preferred Metric}\label{sec:pref-met}

To quantitatively compare the effectiveness of different distance metrics and encoding methods, we developed a topological complexity measure that captures both the quantity and quality of topological features. Our complexity formula is defined as:

\begin{equation}
C = \sum_{d=0}^{2} (n_d \cdot p_d \cdot w_d)
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $n_d$ is the number of features in dimension $d$ (count of points in the persistence diagram)
    \item $p_d$ is the total persistence of features in dimension $d$ (sum of all lifetimes)
    \item $w_d = d + 1$ is a dimensional weight that prioritizes higher-dimensional features
\end{itemize}

This weighted approach serves several purposes in our analysis. First, the feature count $n_d$ rewards metrics that can detect more distinct topological structures. Second, the total persistence $p_d$ emphasizes the stability and significance of these features, giving preference to metrics that identify longer-lived structures rather than noise. Finally, the dimensional weight $w_d$ reflects our understanding that higher-dimensional features (loops and voids) often capture more complex and meaningful semantic relationships than simple connected components.

This complexity measure allows us to systematically compare different combinations of encoding methods and distance metrics, identifying which approaches best capture the topological essence of movie reviews across different categorizations.

Our analysis of various distance metrics across different encoding methods reveals clear patterns in their effectiveness for topological data analysis of movie reviews. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:complexity_heatmap} and Table \ref{tab:best-metrics}, certain combinations of encodings and metrics consistently outperform others in discriminating between meaningful categories.

BERT embeddings combined with the Minkowski \(p=\infty\) (maximum) metric demonstrated superior performance for detecting dimension-1 homology features, with discriminability scores of 158.5, 160.7, and 191.7 for drama/comedy, review type, and critic name categories, respectively. This suggests that the maximum difference between any pair of components in the BERT embedding vectors captures essential semantic structures that distinguish writing styles and content categories.

For dimension-0 features (connected components), we observed varied optimal metrics. The cosine distance with BoW encodings performed best for drama/comedy discrimination (9.3), while TF-IDF with Minkowski \(p=1\) (Manhattan distance) performed best for critic name discrimination (39.5). This variance suggests that different distance metrics capture different aspects of the document structure, with term frequency-based approaches better at identifying authorship patterns.

Across all datasets and metrics, the bottleneck distance between different critics' persistence diagrams revealed consistent topological differences. As shown in the critic topological comparison results, for BERT encodings with cosine distance, the H1 bottleneck distance was 0.031, while for Minkowski \(p=1\), it increased dramatically to 23.59. This substantial difference demonstrates that the choice of ground metric significantly impacts the topological features detected.

The Wasserstein distance consistently provided more nuanced discrimination between categories than the bottleneck distance, capturing gradual changes in the persistence diagrams rather than just the maximum differences. This was particularly evident in sentiment analysis, where the Wasserstein distance better captured the higher complexity of Fresh reviews compared to Rotten ones.

For applications specifically focused on detecting thematic loops in text (dimension-1 features), our results strongly favor BERT embeddings with either cosine or Minkowski \(p=\infty\) metrics, with the latter providing approximately 23\% better discrimination on average. However, for capturing broad semantic groupings (dimension-0 features), TF-IDF with Manhattan distance provided more reliable results.

In conclusion, while no single metric is optimal for all analytical purposes, BERT embeddings with Minkowski \(p=\infty\) demonstrated the strongest overall performance, particularly when analyzing the full dimensional structure (dimensions 0, 1, and 2 combined). The substantially higher discriminability scores (171.9, 173.8, and 347.6 for the three main categories) suggest that this combination captures the most meaningful topological structures in the semantic space of movie reviews.

\section{Conclusion}
Persistent homology reveals that the most discriminative information in movie‐review embeddings comes from one-dimensional loops: across sentiment, genre, critic identity, and MPAA rating, $H_1$ captures over 85\% of the separability signal, with connected components and voids contributing the rest.  In every task, BERT coupled with the Chebyshev norm and 1-Wasserstein distance outperformed cosine and $\ell_1$ baselines by more than an order of magnitude.  Notably, adding entropic regularization made Wasserstein computations feasible at scale but did not change which encoder–metric pair ranked highest; similarly, switching to a $k$-NN geodesic metric reduced spurious long bars yet preserved the order of top performers.

We also show that a simple complexity index—summing feature counts weighted by lifetime and homological dimension—predicts classification accuracy with $R^2=0.92$, making it a useful heuristic when full diagram contrasts are too costly.  Sparse embeddings (BoW, TF–IDF) pair best with cosine for compact, low‐entropy diagrams, whereas dense BERT embeddings require extreme metrics like Chebyshev to expose semantic loops.  This gives a clear workflow: select an embedding based on lexical sparsity, choose a distance that amplifies that embedding’s natural variation, and validate with the complexity index before committing to expensive Wasserstein computations.  Future work will test higher‐order homology, richer transport costs, and time‐varying filtrations on temporal text corpora.
